## Full Draft

### Introduction

If you're a South Asian man, there's a good chance you've sat in a doctor's office and been told your lab results are "borderline" or "normal" — and walked out with nothing more than a suggestion to eat better and exercise more.

And yet South Asian men develop type 2 diabetes at nearly 4 times the rate of white Americans. We develop it younger. We develop it at lower body weights. And a growing body of clinical evidence makes clear that the screening thresholds most American physicians use were never validated for our population.

This isn't a medical criticism of your doctor. It's a data problem that most clinical practice hasn't caught up to yet.

Here's what you need to know.

---

### Why Standard BMI Cutoffs Don't Apply to South Asians

The clinical threshold for metabolic risk screening in the United States is a BMI of 30 — the standard definition of obesity. Below that, most guidelines suggest minimal intervention unless other risk factors are present.

The problem: South Asians develop metabolic complications at a BMI of 23.

Both the World Health Organization and the American Diabetes Association have acknowledged lower BMI thresholds for South Asian populations. The ADA's 2023 Standards of Care recommends screening South Asians for type 2 diabetes starting at a BMI of 23, regardless of other risk factors.

A South Asian man at a BMI of 26 — which American clinical guidelines would call "overweight" with no urgent intervention needed — may already have significant visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and be on a trajectory toward type 2 diabetes within 5–10 years.

But if your BMI is 26 and your A1c is 5.6%, most primary care visits will tell you you're fine.

You might not be.

---

### The Visceral Fat Problem

South Asians tend to carry fat disproportionately in the visceral (abdominal) compartment, even at relatively low overall body weights. This is sometimes called the "thin-fat" phenotype — normal scale weight, but elevated metabolic risk due to where fat is stored.

Visceral fat is metabolically active in a dangerous way: it drives insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and chronic inflammation at levels that subcutaneous fat does not.

Waist circumference is a better proxy for visceral fat than BMI. The South Asian-specific risk threshold for waist circumference in men is **90 cm** — compared to the standard American guideline of 102 cm.

A 12-centimeter difference. If your doctor is using the standard cutoff and you're South Asian, you may be carrying clinically meaningful visceral fat that's simply not being flagged.

---

### The A1c Gray Zone

A1c of 5.7–6.4% is the clinical prediabetes range. An A1c below 5.7% is classified as normal.

Two problems:

**First**, some South Asians develop significant insulin resistance before A1c rises into the prediabetes range. A1c reflects average blood glucose over ~90 days, but early metabolic dysfunction often manifests first in postprandial (post-meal) glucose spikes and insulin dysregulation — neither of which is captured by a standard A1c.

**Second**, there is evidence that South Asians may have a higher rate of complications at any given A1c than non-SA populations — partly because diagnosis often comes later in the disease process, after years of undetected insulin resistance.

The appropriate response to a 5.6% A1c in a South Asian man with a family history of diabetes and a waist circumference of 91cm is not "you're fine, come back next year." It's a comprehensive metabolic workup.

---

### What a Proper Metabolic Workup Looks Like

Standard annual blood panels typically include: A1c, fasting glucose, basic lipid panel (total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides), and a basic metabolic panel.

Here's what a South Asian-specific metabolic workup adds:

| Test | Why It Matters for SA Men |
|------|--------------------------|
| **Fasting insulin** | Lets you calculate HOMA-IR — the gold-standard estimate of insulin resistance. Normal fasting glucose can mask high insulin resistance. |
| **HOMA-IR** | Calculated from fasting glucose and insulin. Score > 2.5 suggests meaningful insulin resistance in most populations; SA-specific reference ranges may be even lower. |
| **ApoB** | Better predictor of cardiovascular risk than LDL alone. Elevated ApoB is common in SA men even with "normal" LDL. |
| **hsCRP** | High-sensitivity C-reactive protein. Marker of systemic inflammation — a key driver of metabolic disease progression. |
| **Lp(a)** | Lipoprotein(a) — a genetic cardiovascular risk factor that is elevated in South Asians at higher rates than other populations. Not routinely screened. |
| **ALT/AST** | Screen for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), which co-occurs with metabolic syndrome and is more common in SA men. |
| **Waist circumference** | Assessed to SA threshold (>90cm), not standard US threshold (>102cm). |

None of this is exotic. All of these are standard lab tests. The question is whether your doctor is ordering them and interpreting them in your population context.

---

### The Follow-Up Gap

Even when prediabetes is identified, the most common management approach in primary care is lifestyle counseling at an annual visit. "Eat less sugar. Exercise more. We'll check again next year."

For South Asian men, who progress from prediabetes to diabetes faster than most populations, annual follow-up is often not enough.

Intensive lifestyle intervention — the kind studied in the Diabetes Prevention Program, which showed 58% reduction in diabetes progression — requires sustained engagement, not one conversation per year.

---

### What to Ask Your Doctor

If you're a South Asian man with any of the following — family history of diabetes, waist circumference > 90cm, A1c 5.4% or above, or unexplained fatigue/weight gain — ask for:

1. Fasting insulin (same blood draw as fasting glucose, just add it on)
2. HOMA-IR calculation
3. ApoB
4. Waist circumference measured to SA-specific threshold

If your current doctor isn't familiar with South Asian-specific metabolic risk, you may need to find one who is.
