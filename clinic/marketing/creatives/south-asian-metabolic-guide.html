The Problem

"Your tests came back normal."
But something still feels off.

South Asian men are developing prediabetes and metabolic disease at twice the rate of their white peers — often despite healthier diets and more exercise. By age 45, nearly 1 in 3 South Asian men has prediabetes. Most of them have no idea.

The standard labs miss the story

When your doctor orders an A1c, they're looking at a three-month average of your blood sugar. It's useful. But it's a lagging indicator — by the time it moves, insulin resistance has often been building for a decade.

The thresholds your doctor uses were calibrated on European populations. For South Asian men, the danger zones start earlier and look different. Most physicians weren't taught this. It's not negligence — it's a gap in the evidence base.

This guide gives you what most standard blood panels leave out.

The South Asian Metabolic Paradox

Healthier habits. Worse outcomes. Why?

South Asians — including men who exercise, maintain a healthy weight, and avoid processed food — disproportionately develop insulin resistance, prediabetes, and cardiovascular disease. Understanding why requires looking past the standard metrics.

Visceral fat vs. total body fat

South Asians carry a higher proportion of visceral fat (the fat surrounding internal organs) relative to their total body fat compared to other ethnic groups. This fat is metabolically active in ways that subcutaneous fat is not — it drives insulin resistance, inflammation, and cardiovascular risk.

A South Asian man at BMI 24 — "healthy" by standard charts — may carry the visceral fat burden of a white man at BMI 28. The BMI number doesn't capture this.

"A South Asian man at BMI 24 may have the cardiovascular risk equivalent of a White man at BMI 28."
— MASALA Study, University of California San Francisco

Insulin resistance develops earlier

Research from the MASALA Study — the largest longitudinal study of South Asian cardiovascular health in the US — shows that South Asian men develop insulin resistance earlier, at lower BMI, and often without the warning signs that flag risk in other populations.

The 3 Tests Your Doctor Probably Didn't Order

A1c is a lagging indicator.
Here's what to ask for instead.

These three tests are not exotic or experimental — they're standard lab tests available through any major lab. Most primary care physicians simply don't order them routinely. For South Asian men, they can reveal insulin resistance years before A1c moves.

1. Fasting Insulin Ask for this
Optimal range: 2–6 µIU/mL  ·  Most labs flag "normal" up to 25

The single most important early-warning test for insulin resistance. Your pancreas pumps out extra insulin to keep blood sugar normal — so A1c looks fine while insulin is quietly tripling. Fasting insulin catches this early.

2. HOMA-IR Calculate from results
Threshold for concern: > 2.0  ·  Standard labs often don't report this

Calculated from fasting glucose and fasting insulin: (Fasting Insulin × Fasting Glucose) ÷ 405. South Asian men often show HOMA-IR > 3.5 with a "normal" A1c of 5.4. Ask your doctor to calculate it, or do it yourself.

3. ApoB (Apolipoprotein B) Better than LDL
Optimal: < 80 mg/dL  ·  South Asians often show high ApoB with "normal" LDL

LDL cholesterol undercounts cardiovascular risk in South Asians, who tend to have smaller, denser LDL particles. ApoB counts every atherogenic particle directly — it's a more accurate predictor. Ask your doctor to add it to your lipid panel.

The Complete Picture

The other 5 markers worth knowing

Beyond the 3 critical tests, a complete metabolic evaluation for South Asian men should include these additional markers. Together, they give a picture that a standard annual blood panel cannot.

Marker Why It Matters South Asian Threshold
Waist circumference The best single proxy for visceral fat. BMI misses it. Measure at the navel, not the belt. > 90 cm (35.5 in) — vs. 102 cm standard
Triglyceride : HDL ratio A strong proxy for insulin resistance. Often called the "poor man's insulin test." Free from your standard lipid panel. > 2.0 is a warning sign
Uric acid Elevated in insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome. Often ignored. Linked to gout, kidney stones, and cardiovascular disease. > 5.5 mg/dL warrants attention
Ferritin Often elevated with metabolic syndrome and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Frequently dismissed unless very high. > 200 µg/L without explanation
hsCRP High-sensitivity C-reactive protein. Marker of chronic low-grade inflammation — a driver of both insulin resistance and cardiovascular disease. > 1.0 mg/L worth investigating

How to request them

All 8 tests are available through standard labs (Quest, LabCorp, etc.). Your physician can order them — most won't proactively. At your next annual physical, bring the conversation guide on page 6 and ask directly.

Note: Many PCPs haven't incorporated South Asian-specific thresholds into their practice. If your results fall in "normal" ranges by standard criteria but within the South Asian risk zones, you may need to advocate for follow-up or seek a specialist.

Self-Assessment

Where do you stand?

Check any items that apply to you. This is not a diagnostic tool — it's a way to gauge whether a full South Asian-specific metabolic evaluation is worth pursuing.

  • A1c between 5.5–5.9% ("normal" or "borderline")
  • Fasting glucose 95–115 mg/dL
  • BMI 22–26 with abdominal fat you can't seem to lose
  • One or both parents with type 2 diabetes
  • A sibling with type 2 diabetes or insulin resistance
  • Triglycerides > 150 mg/dL
  • Fatigue, brain fog, or energy crashes after meals
  • Difficulty losing weight despite consistent exercise

Doctor Visit Prep

What to ask at your
next appointment

Print this page and bring it. These are direct, evidence-based requests — not fringe requests. Every test listed is standard medicine. The difficulty is that most physicians don't order them proactively.

Script — say or show this to your doctor

"I'd like to add fasting insulin and ApoB to my lab panel. Can you also calculate my HOMA-IR from the results? I've read that South Asians develop insulin resistance at lower BMI and lower A1c thresholds — and I'd like to be proactive about checking the full picture while I'm still in a normal range."

If they're unfamiliar with HOMA-IR:

"HOMA-IR is calculated as (Fasting Insulin × Fasting Glucose) ÷ 405. It's not a special test — just a formula from the two values I'm already requesting. I can calculate it myself from the results if needed."

If they say your A1c is fine:

"I understand A1c is normal. I'm concerned about early insulin resistance, which can be present for 5–10 years before A1c moves. Fasting insulin is the early-warning marker I'm looking for."

If they're resistant:

You are allowed to ask twice. If your doctor dismisses metabolic concerns that are evidence-based and low-risk to test for, you deserve a second opinion. The tests cost $50–150 out of pocket and are available directly through Quest or LabCorp without a physician order in most states.

What to do with your results:

Most labs will give you a printout or portal access. If your fasting insulin is >10 µIU/mL, HOMA-IR >2.0, or ApoB >80 mg/dL, consider a consultation with a physician who understands South Asian metabolic risk specifically.
