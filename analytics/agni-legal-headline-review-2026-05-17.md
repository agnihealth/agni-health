# Agni Health — Hero Headline Legal Review
**Date:** 2026-05-17  
**Reviewer:** clinic-legal (subagent)  
**Scope:** Website/landing page compliance — FTC, FDA, telehealth marketing (CA, LA, MA)  
**Not in scope:** Meta/Facebook ad policy

---

## Legal Framework Summary

- **FTC:** All claims must be truthful and substantiated by competent, reliable scientific evidence. Health claims cannot imply efficacy beyond what the evidence supports.
- **FDA:** For a licensed medical practice marketing *consultations* (not a drug/device/supplement), FDA jurisdiction is limited. The key risk is **implied disease claims** — language that implies diagnosing, mitigating, treating, or preventing a specific disease can trigger FDA scrutiny even for practice marketing.
- **State telehealth (CA, LA, MA):** Licensed physician can advertise specialties and describe conditions treated. Cannot make guarantees, imply superior outcomes, or imply that standard-of-care medicine is failing patients in ways that could be seen as deceptive.
- **Footer disclaimer** (prescription required, medical evaluation needed, individual results vary) provides meaningful but not unlimited protection — it cannot cure a facially misleading headline.

## Headline Reviews

---

### DEFAULT: "Standard 'normal' wasn't built / for South Asian biology"

**Verdict: ✅ Legally clear**

**Analysis:**  
This is commentary on population-specific medical reference ranges — a well-substantiated area of medical literature. Research consistently shows that standard BMI cutoffs, glucose thresholds, and metabolic risk stratifications were developed on predominantly European populations and underperform for South Asian patients. This is not a disease claim, not a treatment efficacy claim, and not an individual diagnostic. It positions Agni Health as addressing a recognized gap in standard medicine.

**Risk level:** Low. The claim is directional and population-level, not individual.

---

### VARIANT 1: "South Asian bodies store fat / differently"

**Verdict: ✅ Legally clear**

**Analysis:**  
Factual and well-substantiated. Peer-reviewed research extensively documents differential visceral adiposity patterns, fat distribution, and metabolic phenotyping in South Asian populations (e.g., Misra & Vikram, 2004; Anand et al., 2000; WHO Expert Consultation 2004). This is a structure/function claim about human biology, not a disease or treatment claim. No modification required.

**Risk level:** Low.

---

### VARIANT 2: "Your metabolic risk is higher / than standard guidelines recognize"

**Verdict: ⚠️ Needs modification**

**Analysis:**  
The underlying claim is substantiated — South Asians develop type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at lower BMI thresholds and younger ages than standard clinical guidelines account for. However, **"Your metabolic risk is higher"** directed at an individual visitor is an implicit individual health assessment. Without a clinical evaluation, Agni Health cannot determine whether *this specific visitor's* metabolic risk is elevated. This edges toward unlicensed remote diagnosis and could expose the practice to FTC "deceptive claims" liability if a visitor interprets it as a personal assessment.

**Rewrite:**  
> *"South Asian metabolic risk is higher / than standard guidelines recognize"*

This preserves the marketing punch — validating why visitors should care — while framing the claim at the population level where it's substantiated.

---

### VARIANT 3: "Your blood sugar looks fine. / It's not the whole story"

**Verdict: ⚠️ Needs modification**

**Analysis:**  
This is the highest-risk headline in the set. The construction "Your blood sugar looks fine" presupposes the visitor has had labs reviewed and they appear normal. "It's not the whole story" implies the visitor has an undiagnosed metabolic condition their current doctor missed. This combination:

1. **Creates an implied disease claim** — suggesting undiagnosed subclinical metabolic disease
2. **Could be read as criticizing prior medical care**, raising state medical board concerns in CA and MA, which prohibit advertising that demeans other practitioners
3. **"Your"** makes this an individualized statement directed at the visitor, not a general population statement

The footer disclaimer does not fix a headline that individually implies "your normal labs are hiding a problem." A plaintiff's attorney could argue this induced the visitor to believe they had an undiagnosed condition.

**Rewrite:**  
> *"Normal blood sugar doesn't always tell / the full story for South Asian bodies"*

Removes the individualized diagnostic implication, keeps the scientific substance (HbA1c and fasting glucose can miss early insulin resistance), and doesn't imply criticism of prior care.

---

### VARIANT 4: "Your risk starts / earlier than you think"

**Verdict: ✅ Legally clear (minor note)**

**Analysis:**  
"Risk" in a general health marketing context is acceptable and widely used. The claim is substantiated: South Asians develop type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease at younger ages and lower BMI thresholds than the general population. The phrase "earlier than you think" is comparative but refers to a documented epidemiological reality.

**Minor note:** "Your" is a mild concern (individualization) but within acceptable range for health practice marketing. Optionally, adding "South Asian" reinforces the substantiation basis: *"Your South Asian risk starts earlier than you think."* Not legally required.

**Risk level:** Low.

---

### VARIANT 5: "Built to address visceral fat / Built for South Asian biology"

**Verdict: ✅ Legally clear**

**Analysis:**  
A licensed medical practice can describe what conditions or biomarkers it specializes in treating. "Address visceral fat" is descriptive of a service focus, not a guarantee of outcomes. Visceral fat is a recognized clinical biomarker (not a disease per se), and a physician practice can lawfully market around metabolic health. The parallel "Built for South Asian biology" anchors the specialty claim. No disease claim, no efficacy guarantee, no individual diagnostic.

**Risk level:** Low-moderate. Ensure the practice is actually providing visceral fat-related metabolic assessment (DEXA, waist circumference protocols, etc.) — FTC requires substantiation that the service actually "addresses" what it claims to address.

---

### VARIANT 6: "A doctor who actually gets it / because he lived it too"

**Verdict: ✅ Legally clear (conditional)**

**Analysis:**  
Physician personal narrative is a legitimate and common form of medical practice marketing. No disease claim, no treatment efficacy claim. The FTC's testimonial rules apply here: the claim "he lived it too" must be **factually accurate** — if the physician is South Asian and has personally navigated metabolic health concerns, this is clear. If it's embellished, it becomes a deceptive claim under FTC guidelines.

**Condition:** Confirm the "lived it too" narrative is factually accurate and documentable. No legal issue if true.

**Risk level:** Low, assuming accuracy.

---

### VARIANT 7: "Finally, weight loss advice / built for your body"

**Verdict: ⚠️ Needs modification**

**Analysis:**  
Two issues:

1. **"Finally" is a comparative superiority claim.** The FTC requires substantiation for superiority claims. "Finally" implies prior weight loss approaches failed the visitor and this one will succeed — that needs more substantiation than a general medical consultation can reliably promise.

2. **"Weight loss advice"** is problematic for a licensed medical practice. It sounds like a diet program or wellness product, not medical care. FTC has specific scrutiny for weight loss claims; more importantly, characterizing physician-supervised metabolic management as "advice" undersells the clinical offering and may create liability if outcomes don't match what "advice" implies. It also doesn't invoke the physician supervision that gives the practice its legal standing for GLP-1 discussions.

**Rewrite (option A — preferred):**  
> *"Metabolic health care / built for South Asian bodies"*

**Rewrite (option B — if weight loss framing is essential for the ad variant):**  
> *"Weight management, finally designed / for South Asian biology"*

Option B retains "finally" anchored to the biological/guideline gap (more defensible than an implied efficacy promise) and frames it as "management" (clinical) vs. "advice" (diet-program adjacent).
